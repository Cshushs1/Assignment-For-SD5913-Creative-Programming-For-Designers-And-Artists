# Why Are We Here?

From the earliest experiments, to wider adoption and increasingly fluent use, and now to a period of rapid acceleration, AI has entered creative and professional work much faster than I originally expected. AI can already generate working code from natural-language instructions, and there is little doubt that this ability will continue to improve.

As a student with a background in illustration, I am not good at learning programming because I do not want to become a professional programmer. In fact, I also do not think that, as humans, it is really ridiculous that we need to compete with AI over who can write code faster.

What I actually want is something more connected to practical creative experience. I want to use programming to expand what I can do as a designer: building a complete and polished personal website, developing small language tools or AI assistants, working with data and creating high-quality interactive projects, and occasionally making lightweight games that can express my own thoughts and ideas.

For me, learning programming is therefore not about how much syntax I can memorize. It is about understanding the logic behind digital products, learning how to translate a design idea into a system that can actually run, and being able to judge whether an AI-generated result is really what I want.

> AI makes it easier for me to make things, but programming gives me the ability to understand, modify, and control what I am making instead of simply accepting the result AI gives me.

## When AI Helped Me Cross the Coding Barrier

My background is mainly in art and design rather than computer science. In the past, I could design a website’s visual system, page structure, and interaction prototype in Figma, but there was always a technical gap between “I designed a website” and “I built a website that actually works.”

Later, when I started using ChatGPT, Cursor, and WorkBuddy, that gap became much smaller. ChatGPT helped me understand errors and parts of the code logic, while Cursor and WorkBuddy helped me generate, modify, and implement website functions.

For example, I once experimented with building a website for phone cases. From page design to implementing several basic functions, it took only around twenty to thirty minutes before AI could help turn it into something that actually worked. In some parts of the process, I did not even need to rebuild the page manually. AI could continue generating and implementing the website directly based on my Figma design.

For someone with very limited programming experience, this was much easier than I had imagined. Things that once felt beyond my abilities suddenly became achievable.

At the same time, however, I encountered a strange problem:

**The website could already run, but I did not fully understand why it worked.**

For example, I could not clearly explain how its language switching or currency conversion actually worked. If I wanted to turn this experiment into a real product, I also did not know how these functions should connect to email, payment systems, or other services.

This experience made me feel, for the first time, that I urgently needed to change the question I had been asking.

> Can AI write code for me?

Clearly, it can.

The real question became:

> If AI can already help me quickly build a polished website, why do I still need to learn programming?

## Describing Is Not the Same as Specifying

AI is very good at understanding natural-language instructions. I can say:

> “Make this website more interactive while keeping a strong visual aesthetic.”

Or:

> “Make the animations and click feedback feel smoother.”

It can usually give me a result.

But **getting a result does not mean getting the result I actually want.**

What does “smoother” really mean?

A shorter animation? Faster feedback after a click? A more natural transition between pages? Or less information appearing at the same time?

When I move from websites into small tools, data projects, or lightweight games, this issue becomes even more obvious.

I still need to decide what information the user enters, how the data is processed, what happens after a button is clicked, what conditions trigger the next state, and how different user behaviors produce different results.

AI can suggest answers to these questions, and it can even generate a complete solution. But in the end, I still need to decide: **which solution actually fits the experience I want to create?**

> AI lowers the cost of producing code, but it does not remove the need for precise design decisions.

For me, one important reason to learn programming is that it forces me to turn:

> “I want it to work roughly like this.”

into something much clearer:

> **rules, states, conditions, and logic.**

In other words, programming helps me turn a vague design idea into a system that can actually run while still remaining something I can understand and control.

## Reading May Become More Important Than Writing

As AI continues to improve, I expect to manually write less code.

A traditional workflow might look like:

> Write → Run → Debug

My future workflow may look more like:

> Describe → Generate → Read → Test → Evaluate → Modify

I may not need to memorize every syntax rule or begin every project from a blank file.

But this does not mean I no longer need to understand code.

If AI gives me hundreds of lines of code that I cannot understand, whenever something breaks my only option is to give the error back to AI and hope that it fixes it next time.

On the surface, I can still say:

> “I built this website.”

But in reality, how much of it do I actually control?

> If I cannot understand a system well enough to question it, evaluate it, or change it, can I really say that I designed it?

For me, programming literacy may therefore become less about writing everything manually and more about reading, debugging, testing, evaluating, and modifying systems.

## Programming Expands What I Can Design

Programming also matters because it changes what I imagine is possible.

If I mainly work with traditional visual design tools, I may naturally think:

> Poster → Page → Interface → Website

Programming expands that range:

> Interactive Website → Small Digital Tool → Data-driven Experience → Lightweight Game

A website can respond differently to different user inputs. A useful dataset can quickly become an interactive experience. A very simple interaction can gradually develop into a game rule.

These are not only technical changes. They also change the kinds of design ideas I can imagine in the first place.

Dylan Beattie’s *The Art of Code* also made me think about programming differently. His examples show code not only as a way of solving technical problems, but also as something creative and expressive.

At the same time, AI has made programming feel less like a completely separate technical world that I need to struggle to enter, and more like **another creative material that I can learn how to use.**

This also connects to Don Ihde’s discussion in *Technology and the Lifeworld*. Technology is not simply a neutral tool; it also influences how we experience and understand the world.

I feel something similar happening as I learn programming. Once I begin to understand how digital systems work, I also start imagining projects that I would not have thought of before.

Programming expands my **design vocabulary**.

It does not only help me realize ideas I already have. **It also gives me more inspiration and more possibilities for actually making those ideas real.**

## What Might Actually Become Obsolete?

I do think some parts of traditional programming may become less important, especially for someone like me who comes from a more traditional art and design background.

Memorizing large amounts of syntax, manually writing repetitive code, or building basic standard pages from scratch may become increasingly unnecessary when AI can do these things in seconds.

If programming education only teaches students to repeat syntax more accurately than a machine, I am not sure that this kind of learning will have the same value in the future.

What matters may simply change:

> Syntax ↓  

> System Thinking ↑  

> Specification ↑  

> Debugging ↑  

> Judgement ↑  

> Understanding ↑

This does not mean syntax has no value.

I still need to understand enough syntax to read code, recognize basic structures, and understand roughly what is happening inside a system.

For me, **syntax is no longer the final goal of learning. It is a means of understanding systems.**

I do not want to learn programming to compete with AI at producing code.

I want to learn **how to understand systems built from code and how to make my own judgments and decisions about them.**

## Why Am I Here?

I do not expect programming to become the center of my future design practice, and I do not want it to become the center.

As my opinion, the most important thing is still the **idea**(including emotion, intention, and judgment).

What kind of experience do I want to create? What do I want people to feel or understand when they use it? Why should this project exist at all?

What I value most in the creative process is the ability to form ideas, make judgments, and decide what an experience should ultimately become. I do not think learning programming will replace any of these things.

Instead, it simply gives these ideas another way to become real.

In the future, I want to use programming to build personal websites, small digital tools, data and interaction projects, and lightweight games. I also believe that more and more of the code behind these projects will be generated with the help of AI.

But this does not make programming less worth learning.

**It simply changes what I think is most worth learning from programming.**

I do not need to compete with a machine over who can write code faster.

What I really need is to understand the medium well enough to know what I actually want, turn a vague idea into clear system logic, recognize when an AI-generated answer is wrong, generic, or simply unsuitable for my design, and know how to continue modifying it.

Learning programming is not about letting technology replace my creative process.

Instead, it is about making sure that even when AI becomes part of that creative process, the important decisions are still made by me.

> AI can help me realize an idea faster, but what to create, why to create it, and what it should ultimately become should still be decisions that I make.

That is why I am here.
